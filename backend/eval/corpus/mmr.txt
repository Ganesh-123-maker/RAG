Maximal Marginal Relevance for Diverse Retrieval

Dense retrieval ranks chunks by similarity to the query and returns
the top results. The problem is that the most similar chunks are
often near-duplicates of each other: several chunks from the same
paragraph, or several chunks repeating the same sentence with small
changes. A top-5 list full of near-duplicates wastes slots that could
have covered different aspects of the question.

Maximal Marginal Relevance, shortened to MMR, fixes this by balancing
relevance against diversity. MMR builds the final list one chunk at a
time. At each step it picks the candidate that maximises a combined
score: lambda times the candidate's relevance score, minus
one-minus-lambda times the candidate's highest similarity to any
chunk already selected. The first term rewards relevance; the second
term penalises redundancy.

The parameter lambda controls the balance. This guide uses a lambda
value of 0.7, which weights relevance more heavily than diversity:
a candidate must be substantially redundant before a slightly less
relevant but different candidate wins. A lambda of 1.0 would ignore
diversity entirely and reproduce plain similarity ranking, while a
lambda of 0.0 would ignore relevance and return the most diverse
set regardless of whether it answers the question.

MMR needs a candidate pool larger than the final list. This guide
fetches a pool of 20 candidates and selects a final top-k of 5 from
it. Fetching only 5 candidates and then applying MMR would be
pointless, because there would be nothing to choose between. The
pool size is therefore a real parameter, not an implementation
detail: a larger pool gives diversity more room to work, at the cost
of more similarity computations.

MMR measures redundancy with cosine similarity between candidate
vectors. That requires the candidate vectors themselves, not just
their scores. If a vector store returns scores without vectors, MMR
cannot measure redundancy and must fall back to plain score order.
That fallback should be visible in logs, because silently returning
score order while claiming diversity would mislead anyone tuning the
lambda parameter.
